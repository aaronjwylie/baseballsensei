Eighteen steps, and what happens on each

Eighteen steps — a block is a status, and everything a person does while sitting on it is inside that block's chain. Uploading isn't a stage that follows verification; it is what step 2 is. Read a band left to right: whose court the ball is in, what is already true, what moved it here, the work still to do, and one line written as finished — the thing that ends the rung. Everything left of that line is outstanding, which is how a rung a submission is actually sitting on ought to read. Eighteen steps, sixteen statuses — the two hand-offs to a translator share a status with the work that follows them, which is the one place a block is not a status. The line that matters is step 3 — before payment a submission is a scratch pad; after it, a record.

Five paths that aren't stages

The ladder runs 1 → 18. These branch off it and can't be numbered.

  1. AA declined card — branches from step 4. A decline is a customer trying, not a customer leaving: nothing rolls back, the files stay, retrying is one tap, and the attempt buys them time rather than letting the window run out underneath them. They're emailed a way back in. ⚠ Today nobody is emailed and the clock keeps running.
  2. BThe address bounces — branches from step 2. Measured at ~2 seconds after the send, and the customer is by then looking at a code input for a message that will never arrive. Nothing can push it to them, so two things surface it: a single delayed check five seconds in — not a poll, because a two-second failure doesn't need one — and their next action, since typing a code or asking for a new one both check first. Either returns them to step 1, with wording that depends on the kind of bounce: hard means the address doesn't exist, soft means the inbox couldn't take it (full, or refusing us), and an unrecognised classification gets wording true of both. Telling someone with a full mailbox to check for a typo sends them hunting for a mistake they didn't make. It scrubs nothing, and can't need to: a bounce of ① can only happen before verification, and uploading requires verification, so there are never any files. ⚠ After payment a bounce does nothing automatic — a failing receipt is real and it is the admin's, and nothing acts destructively on a submission somebody paid for.
  3. CThe window lapses, or the guesses run out — branches from steps 2, 3 or 4. Two triggers, one outcome, and it is exactly the outcome of refreshing the page: the scratch pad is scrubbed, row and bytes together, and the customer is returned to step 1 with a sentence explaining why. Never left standing on a step whose submission no longer exists. Five wrong guesses is terminal, not resettable — there is no “new code, five more tries”, because the submission is gone. It costs little: uploads are gated on verification, so at step 2 only typed detail exists. ⚠ Not built — today a lapse surfaces as an inline error and the customer stays put.
  4. DAdmin intervenes — available from step 4 onward. The pipeline runs forward on its own; this is the handle for when it shouldn't. Two admin-only powers, both deliberately blunt: purge the folders (delete any or all of the four file sets now, without waiting for a clock) and reset the status (move a submission back to an earlier rung). This is the answer to “what can be undone” — one general handle beats eleven per-stage undo buttons nobody remembers exist. If Admin isn't satisfied with a coach's work he'll speak to them directly; the system's job is to let him put the submission back, not to model the conversation. Both write to internalNotes, because a submission that moved backwards without explanation is worse than one that didn't move. ⚠ Neither is built.
  5. EChecking status — recurring, any time after step 4, as often as they like. Two ways in: email + a fresh 6-digit PIN mailed each time, or a non-guessable link carried in the receipt. Both grant the same thing: see the status, and once step 14 has run, download. ⚠ Neither is built — today the status page shows results from an unverified email, so anyone guessing an address sees them. This closes that.

The point of no return

“Should a failed stage undo itself?” has no single answer, and looking for one was the mistake. It resolves per operation, and the line is sharp.

An operation must survive a failure if its effect already exists outside our database. It must be undone if the only place it is true is inside.

Undoing what the world already did makes the record lie; keeping what only we believe makes it lie the other way. So every stage has a point of no return — the first operation whose effect escapes us. Before it: scrub, a clean retry is correct. From it on: keep, and repair forward — the world has moved and the database catches up.

Step 4, killed after the card clears

The money moved. Un-marking it paid would be a lie Stripe will happily contradict, and the customer is charged for a submission we claim doesn't exist. Keep everything; repair the missing receipt forward.

Step 13, killed after the stamp, before the email

Nothing has left the building. complete would mean “delivered” while the customer has heard nothing — a claim only we believe. Scrub the stamp and let Admin press the button again.

Two rules that fall out of it

Put the point of no return as late in the chain as you can. Everything before it is cleanly reversible, so the later it sits, the more of the stage is safe.

When both failure states are bad, fail toward the one someone will notice. Step 13 is where the ordering isn't free — the email needs the record. Complete with no email is silent: the queue says delivered, the customer hears nothing, and nobody learns otherwise until a complaint. Not-complete with a stray link is visible: the row sits in the approval queue and the link works or 404s harmlessly. Choose the loud failure.

Read the “none” rows as good news. Five stages have no point of no return at all — they move a status and nothing else — so they're trivially safe to retry. That isn't an accident of implementation; it's what “off-platform” and “internal only” mean.

Step 2's exception is the one thing here that isn't about the outside world. The attempt counter is a ratchet: it increments before the comparison, so abandoning a request still spends one, and a failure must never hand it back. Nothing external changed, but rolling it back turns five guesses into unlimited ones.

Steps 3, 7, 10 and 12 fail in the opposite direction from everything else. The bytes reach storage before the row exists, so a failure leaves the world ahead of the record rather than behind it. Nothing to undo — the repair is to finish writing the row, and the thing to avoid is a cleanup that deletes files a customer successfully sent.

⚠ None of this is enforced today. Every stage is a bare try/catch and the dispositions above are aspirations. The cheapest first move is stage 1, the only one whose residue a customer can currently collide with.

Every substep, in three voices

Every substep of every rung, and what each one leaves behind.
Redrawn 2026-08-03 — the target, not the build. Fifty-six substeps across eighteen steps, twenty-seven of them wholly new. 265 failure rows, 218 success rows, 332 messages. update the client's status lines write one sentence that gets overwritten, not a log: the status page answers “where is my submission” for someone who wondered, and a scrolling history would make them read six lines to learn the one thing they came for. The trail already keeps the history, and it is the operator's tool.

The first three columns are the substep in the operator's own words: what still has to happen, and what it's called once it has. The last three are what gets written down, split apart because the difference between them is what matters when you are reading a submission's history — the row you see on a good run, the row you see on a bad one, who sets it going, and — for each outcome — what a person is actually told. Eighteen of the fifty-six need a person; thirty-eight fire on their own.

The written row and the spoken message are not the same event and do not always both happen. A bounced verification code writes a row and puts a sentence in front of the customer; a refused reassignment does neither. The trail is the record, the message is the experience, and a pipeline is only as good as the worse of the two. Every message names its audience and the surface it lands on — and the customer has two of those, the checkout flow and the status page they sign into afterwards with their email and an access code. The difference is load-bearing: a dead download link is a status-page problem and could never appear in the flow, while a declined card is the reverse. A step writes exactly one status row, on arrival, naming itself — so it sits in the Step column rather than inside any substep. Draft is the first line of every trail; Upload opens the upload step rather than closing the draft. The other three kinds belong to substeps: the send, the check, and the attachment. Under every name in this table is › the row it writes, so a step, a substep opening and a substep closing each account for their own line — which is what makes the trail an outline rather than a list. Attachments are one row per file, enumerated to the cap: the browser sends them separately and any one can fail alone, so a single “3 files attached” would summarise three events that need not all have happened. An email belongs to exactly one substep — the one it lands on, not the one that triggered it. Seven were claimed twice, by the button that sends and the rung that records; pressing Release feedback moves the row to Delivered, and ⑧ is what step 14 is for. A message reaching two parties is two entries: they are two deliveries that can fail independently, and ⑥ reaching the coach but not the admin is a real state that one row saying “both” could not describe.

This is the northstar, not the build. Where nobody is told today, the entry says what should be said and carries not built. Silence is a gap, not the absence of one — and there is a lot of it: every bounced notification to an operator currently surfaces nowhere, and both the operator’s and the coach’s uploads accept anything and report nothing.

That last column is why this is worth six columns rather than a footnote: an empty trail can mean two different things, and only this says which. The strings are verbatim — you can search a trail for a line and find it here, or read it here and know exactly what to look for.

What simulating the ladder found

npm run simulate walks all sixteen rungs twice — with translation and without — through the real domain functions rather than the database, so the guards, the trail and the sends all run. 116 checks. It found three bugs on its first run, and all three were the same shape: a guard written when the ladder was shorter, never widened when it grew.

Hand-offa translated intake could never be handed offit sits at intake_translated, and the guard only accepted assigned. The button appeared, the action returned, nothing happened
Approvaltranslated feedback could never be approvedthe exact mirror. A review could be translated and then never sent
Rungs 5 & 10were unreachablenothing ever wrote intake_translating or response_translating

The third is the one worth remembering. Uploading a translation jumped straight from assigned to intake_translated, so a submission sitting on the admin's laptop for two days was indistinguishable from one he hadn't started — which is precisely what those rungs exist to show.

They need an explicit action, not an inferred one. The download can't be the signal: an admin opens a file to check it as often as to translate it, and guessing intent from a click would send submissions out for translation nobody sent. Hence “Send for translation →”, offered on both sides and marked passive so it never gates the shared-language rows that skip it.

Why nothing else caught them. Predicates fixed this class for the reads — isPaid, isReleased, whoseCourt are exhaustive Records, so a new rung is a compile error. The writes have no equivalent: a guard comparing one literal status stays valid TypeScript forever. No type error, no failing test, and no amount of reading. A simulation was the only thing that could find them.

Why a block is a rung

This used to be seventeen stages against sixteen statuses, and the mismatch went stale exactly where you'd expect. “Files attached” was a stage with no status of its own, so one block had no home and one rung silently carried two jobs — which is how a customer mid-upload came to show as “awaiting payment”. It is now eighteen steps against those same sixteen statuses, and the two that don't line up are named: steps 5 and 11 — the hand-offs to a translator — share intake_translating and response_translating with the work that follows them. That is a real seam, not a tidy one: the queue cannot tell “sent to a translator” from “the translator is working on it” without reading the trail. Left open deliberately, because the alternative is two more rungs on a ladder that is already long.

The doc, this page and the admin queue now render one shape. A rung, then its chain. Two descriptions of one process was the drift.

⚠️ Rung 2 needs renaming. awaiting_payment is the name of its second half; the customer spends most of it uploading. uploading is proposed here and deliberately not migrated — the admin label already says the honest thing, and an enum rename should be taken on its own.

One word per rung, from one map. Sixteen labels sit on a single rail, so none can afford a clause: Draft · Upload · New · Assign · Translating · Translated · Sent · Reviewing · Submitted · Translating · Translated · Deliver · Collected · Resolved · Deleting · Purged. The rung says where; the line beneath it says what is owed, which is what let the names shrink.

Six steps share three names — a submission translates twice, once each way, and “Translate”, “Translating” and “Translated” are honest both times. Position carries the difference on the rail; a flat list can’t, so the override’s dropdown numbers them.

The queue’s filter tabs read from that same map now. They had their own vocabulary until the rename made the two disagree inside one view: a tab called “Sent” filtered everything released, while the rung newly called “Sent” means handed to the coach — which that tab excludes. The cost is that “Not picked up” said someone is waiting on a person, and now the chain line under the pill has to carry it.

It is a path with branches, not a progress bar. Six steps are only touched when a submission needs translating, so a coach who shares a language with the customer takes 4 → 8 and 10 → 14 directly.

Open questions

These are not the gaps. Anything agreed but unbuilt is marked △ in the table above — it needs building, not discussing. What follows is the smaller, harder set: places where nobody could build the thing even with unlimited time, because we haven't decided what right looks like. Four are open, and each came out of writing the substep table rather than from thinking about it in the abstract — which is the pattern every previous round followed too.

  1. 110c and 14b are both Admin approving. On a submission that needs translating, three steps separate them and both are earned — 10c decides whether the coaching is worth paying to translate, 14b picks which set goes out. On the untranslated path they are back to back with nothing in between, and the second press has no choice to make because only one set exists. Either 10c releases directly when no translation is needed, or 10c goes and 14b judges both things at the end — which would mean paying a translator before anyone has read the coaching.
  2. 2Step 9 is still called “Reviewing”. The last use of review anywhere in the document, now that the coach's work is feedback everywhere else. Renaming it is a status-enum change (in_review), so it hasn't been taken lightly. “Writing” fits the row of participles it sits in.
  3. 3Assignment wants a join. assignedCoachId is a single column, and the substep table assumes throughout that more than one coach or translator per submission is normal — every assignment row carries an id rather than a position, precisely so a fourth needs no new row shape. The schema can't hold that yet.
  4. 4Six substeps have no failure column — 15c, 16c, 17c, 18a, 18b, 18d, all in the sweep tail. The claim is that deletion has no failure path because it simply retries on the next sweep. That is probably true and is certainly convenient, which is the combination worth checking rather than assuming.
